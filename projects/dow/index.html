Polymer Quality & Process Intelligence

I led the analysis of a polymer quality-drift problem, identified the process conditions behind it, and built a predictive model from the stronger process signal.

01 / Problem

I started by breaking down what was actually behind the quality drift.

The data came from different points in the production process and at different times. Instead of treating the drift as one correlation problem, I separated the main factors that could affect the result: timing, operating conditions and process history.

The challenge

The process data did not line up cleanly.

Measurements were taken at different times, and the same upstream value could lead to different quality results depending on the process conditions and what had happened earlier.

My approach

I treated the drift as a process problem before treating it as a modeling problem.

I first worked out where the relationship changed and what made it stronger or weaker. That gave me a better base for the deeper analysis and the model that followed.

02 / Analytical design

I built the analysis in stages so each step answered a clear question.

I did not test every method at once. I first aligned the production and quality data, then tested timing, operating conditions and process history. I used what held up through those checks as the basis for the predictive model.

03 / Process analysis

I tested each assumption instead of forcing the data to fit one explanation.

I worked through the same process relationship in different ways: smoothing the data, shifting the timing, separating different operating conditions and adding process history. I kept the changes that improved the signal and dropped the ones that did not.

Process outcome

The drift became a smaller, more focused process problem.

By testing timing, operating conditions and process history separately, I narrowed down what was actually improving the relationship. That gave me a clearer signal to take into the predictive model.

Reuse the process knowledge to predict forward
04 / Predictive model

I turned the stronger process signal into a model for the final quality response.

Once I had a better representation of the process, I used the upstream quality signal, operating conditions and the process-history feature in a multiple regression model. I then used a tree-based model to check which inputs mattered most.

The main result was not that a more complex algorithm performed better. The model improved because I had first built a better representation of the process.

05 / Outcome

I moved the thesis from explaining the drift to predicting the quality response.

The work started with an unclear quality relationship. By testing timing, operating conditions and process history, I found a stronger process signal and carried that learning into the model. The result was a clearer explanation of the drift and a predictive model built on the same process understanding.

What changed

The process relationship became clearer.

Instead of relying on one raw correlation, I showed how timing, operating conditions and process history changed the relationship.

What I built

A model grounded in what I had learned from the process.

The predictive model used the stronger process representation rather than treating modeling as a separate exercise.

From thesis to daily use

The thesis delivered a working predictive model on historical production data. A practical next step would be to run the feature and prediction pipeline on production data, flag emerging quality drift, and support earlier investigation by process engineers. Production deployment and monitoring were outside the scope of the thesis.

Thesis takeaway

Understand the process first. Then model it.

That sequence shaped the whole thesis. I first worked out what was changing the quality relationship, then used that understanding to build the predictive model. The model was the final step, not the starting point.
